DAY 18 · PHASE C MECHANISMS

Evolutionary Developmental Biology

A fertilised egg holds no shapes at all, only a string of instructions run in order — and bodies differ mostly in when those instructions get run

2026-09-11 · BigCat
You and a fly look nothing alike. Yet open up the genes that build each body and it is very nearly the same set. The difference is not which genes are present, but when each is switched on, where, and for how long.

When a building goes up down the street, there is a drawing on site: how high this wall runs, where the window goes, how the pipes are routed. It is hard not to assume a genome is that kind of drawing — every part of a body with its own corresponding line on it.

But a fertilised egg contains no shape whatsoever. It holds a set of instructions executed in time: first decide which end is the head and which the tail, then cut the middle into segments, then let each segment work out what to become. A drawing describes the result; instructions describe only the moves.

In the late 1970s people began breaking those instructions on an industrial scale and looking at the monsters that came out. The monsters gave away the structure of the instructions.

// 01

A body is not built off a drawing — each stretch of it is assigned an identity

Start with two famous monstrous flies. In one, a leg grows where an antenna belongs — not a stump, but a properly jointed leg sticking out of the head. In the other, a second pair of wings grows where a pair of halteres should be (a fly's hindwings are reduced to two small knobs that handle balance in flight), giving a four-winged fly.

Mutations of this kind all land on a small group of genes, later called Hox genes. What matters is how they work: they do not build a leg, and they do not build a wing. Each governs one stretch of the body, and switching it on amounts to telling that stretch "you are the second thoracic segment"; the stretch hears this and calls up the whole wing-building program by itself. So breaking one does not give you a fragment. An entire stretch of body takes on a different identity: the segment that should have made an antenna takes itself for the leg-making segment, and dutifully makes a leg.

This is more like a hotel front desk handing out key cards than like a drawing. The desk does not arrange the furniture; it only tells you that you are on the third floor, and the third floor knows what the third floor looks like. Hand someone the wrong card and they do not lose an arm — they simply walk into a different room.

Then comes the least reasonable part of it. These genes sit in a row along the chromosome, and the order they sit in corresponds one for one with the order of the regions they govern: the ones at this end run the head end, the ones at that end run the tail end. It holds in flies; it holds in mice and in us, where there are four such clusters, thirty-nine genes in all, likewise lined up — and in vertebrates even the timing follows the same order, the earlier ones opening first.

The same eight colours, in the same order along the chromosome as along the body. The order is itself information. (Schematic: a fly's string sits on one chromosome, vertebrates have four such clusters.)

Why they queue up has no agreed answer. And it is not an iron law — in some animals the cluster has broken apart, the correspondence went with it, and their bodies develop perfectly well.

How was this list of instructions compiled? In 1980 Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus used the dumbest method imaginable: generate random mutations in bulk, look at embryos one by one, pull out every one with the wrong number of segments or with scrambled structure inside a segment, and work backwards to the instruction that broke. Together with Lewis, who had studied the four-winged fly earlier, they shared a Nobel Prize in 1995. The list was reconstructed from what broke, not read off from what worked.

// 02

A fly and you are using the same set of switches

A fly's eye is a compound eye assembled from hundreds of small units; yours is a single eye with a lens. Two approaches this far apart, and you would probably guess the genes underneath are separate too.

In 1995 Gehring's lab forced a gene called eyeless to switch on in the legs, wings and antennae of fruit flies, and eye structures grew in those places. Better still, the corresponding mouse gene (named Pax6) put into a fly does the same job — and what grows is still a fly's compound eye, not a mouse's eye.

It is easy to hear this as "the genes for making eyes are shared", which gets it backwards. The mouse gene, inside a fly, starts up the fly's own eye-building program. So it is not a drawing of an eye, it is a switch; what the common ancestor left both sides is that switch and the set of parts hanging under it, not the eye. Structurally the two eyes were very likely built separately, while what they call on underneath is the same ancient kit. This situation is called deep homology.

The same story repeats for appendages. A gene in charge of "push something outward from here" switches on at the corresponding spot in an insect's leg, the margin of a butterfly's wing, the tube feet of a sea urchin and the limb bud of a vertebrate. Anatomically these protruding things have nothing to do with one another; there was no single ancestral limb they all descend from.

Why can such a toolkit sit unchanged for hundreds of millions of years? Because these genes sit at the very top of the program. Change one upstream switch and everything downstream that depends on it changes too, and in the overwhelming majority of cases the embryo does not survive to be born — a variant like that is never passed on even once. So the changes that do accumulate almost never land on the proteins these genes encode. Where do they land instead?

One caveat on how far the line can be pushed. Calling Pax6 "the master switch for eyes" is now regarded as overstatement: it is switched on in plenty of places that never make an eye, and it does not work alone — it sits in a small circle of genes that activate one another.

// 03

Evolution rewires a switch, not the part itself

A giraffe has a nerve that leaves the brain to serve the throat. Brain and throat are less than a hand's width apart, but the nerve does not take that route: it runs down the whole neck into the chest, loops around a major vessel beside the heart, and climbs the whole neck again. Several metres of travel to cover less than a hand's width.

In a fish the same nerve runs straight. A fish has no neck, and that vessel sits near the gills, so the detour costs next to nothing. As necks lengthened generation by generation, the nerve's detour was stretched out along with them. No single generation was worse off — each was only a little longer than the last — and "cut it and reconnect it" is a move no generation could make.

One nerve, one route. In a fish the loop costs almost nothing; once the neck grew long, the same route became several metres of wasted travel. It was not designed this way. It was dragged this way.

Development has no step in which you tear everything down and start over. Each generation's body is modified from the previous generation's running program, and every step along the way has to be an embryo that survives. In 1977 the French biologist François Jacob gave it a phrase still in use: evolution is not an engineer but a tinkerer — a tinkerer designs no parts for the job, uses whatever is at hand, and turns out something that works but rarely looks respectable inside.

So where do the changes usually land? A gene tends to be used at several places in the body and at several times. Changing the protein it encodes changes every one of those uses at once, which is usually a catastrophe. But each individual use is generally controlled by its own stretch of regulatory sequence — think of these as a row of switches at the gene's door, each governing one occasion. Delete one and only that one light goes out.

The cleanest example is the threespine stickleback. Marine sticklebacks carry a pair of hard spines under the belly, and populations stranded in lakes across North America after the ice retreated have lost that pair over and over. On investigation the gene governing the structure is entirely intact, its jobs elsewhere in the body going on as usual; what was deleted is the switch at its door that works only at the pelvis. More telling still, many unconnected lakes deleted the same stretch independently — that sequence is itself prone to breaking. Put it back and the spines come back.

The stronger claim, that morphological evolution is mostly a matter of changing switches, is still contested; others argue that changes in the proteins themselves are systematically underrated. What holds is the weaker version: in the cases worked out so far, what changed was the switch.

Tinkerers' work has one more signature: it keeps pressing one thing into service for something else. The "thumb" a giant panda strips bamboo leaves with is not a finger but an enlarged sesamoid bone in the wrist — all five fingers were already committed. The piece on adaptation and constraint covered historical baggage; here is where the baggage comes from. Not a wrong choice made long ago, but the requirement that every step be completed on a living embryo.

🎒 In Practice · BigCat

  • ① Parenting and education — the fixed evening sequence: homework, bath, story, lights out. You try to move one item earlier and the whole evening falls apart, with every step stalling. This runs the mechanism from "a body is not built off a drawing": the order is itself information, each item triggered by the one before it, so what you moved was not a step but the trigger condition of a string of switches. What to change: don't swap the order directly. First find what actually triggers each item ("bath" is quite possibly "the screen went off"), move that signal to the new position on its own, and only then move the step.
  • ② Leading a team — a handover process was set three years ago, everyone now says it should change, and every attempt stalls halfway: hiring, reconciliation and the on-call rota all hang off it, and none of those can stop for a day. This runs the tinkerer's constraint from "evolution rewires a switch, not the part itself": a system with no maintenance window can only be changed while running, so the workable move is never "new version goes live at once", it is "old and new in parallel for a while". What to change: list the things hanging off it one by one, build each a separate new path and get it working before removing the old one; if you cannot produce the list, don't start.

🌀 Crossing Over · Connections Beyond the Field

  • Engineering and systems design · two unrelated features calling the same old module: in some back office, invoice export and message push look like they have nothing to do with each other, yet you change invoice export and push breaks — both call the same old module. This runs the mechanism from "a fly and you are using the same set of switches": the further upstream something sits and the more places reuse it, the more expensive it is to change the thing itself, so every change that can be shipped safely migrates toward places that take effect at exactly one call site. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise reach: where a system can and cannot be touched has little to do with how well that code is written. Filthy code used in one place is changeable; immaculate code used in twenty places is untouchable. What you should be counting when you assess the risk of a change is call sites, not code quality. But it fails here: call sites in software are explicit, you can find them all at once, and you can swap the underlying module wholesale and fix every caller together. Biology has no such list — how many places a gene is used can only be measured one place at a time — so "this change affects only one spot" is a statistic gathered afterwards, not a guarantee obtained in advance.
  • History and warfare · why an old city's main road takes that inexplicable bend: plenty of old cities have a main road that bends for no stated reason, and when you trace it, there used to be a wall there, or a river, or a burial ground. This runs the tinkerer's constraint from "evolution rewires a switch, not the part itself": rebuilding can only proceed section by section while the city keeps running, and there is no day on which you can shut it down and redraw it, so the lines laid down early become the things every later change routes around. Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise reach: the hardest thing to change in an old city is not the oldest building, it is whatever brings the city to a halt while you touch it — rigidity is set by load-bearing, not by age. A fifty-year-old station can be less movable than a stretch of eight-hundred-year-old wall. But it fails here: cities do occasionally get a bulldozer. Nineteenth-century Paris was cut open and redrawn from above, and a fire or a war can reset the constraints outright. Evolution has no such external power — nobody stands outside a species and presses restart, and every intermediate step has to be a living embryo.

Going Deeper

If the switches are this conserved, how can species differ so much?
The differences come from where the same switches are pressed, how hard and for how long. Finch species in the Galápagos differ widely in how deep and how broad the beak is, and those differences line up with where two or three signalling molecules are active in the embryo and how strongly — not with anyone having an extra "big beak gene".
How are claims about switches actually tested?
Attach a colour-producing marker to the stretch you suspect is a switch, put it into an embryo, and watch where and when it lights up; then delete it and see what is lost, and put it back and see what returns. The stickleback stretch was located and restored exactly that way. The cost is that such experiments can only be done in a few species that are easy to keep and manipulate, so what we know about "how switches change" is naturally biased toward those animals.
Is what changes really always the switch and never the gene itself?
No. One counterexample gets cited often: insects not growing legs on the abdomen is bound up with a change in the protein encoded by a Hox gene itself, not only in a switch. So the safer statement is that changing a switch is cheap and accumulates more easily, but it holds no monopoly.
