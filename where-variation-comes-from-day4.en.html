DAY 4 · PHASE A CORE LOGIC

Where Variation Comes From

Selection can only pick from what is already on the table. How things got onto the table is a different machine altogether

2026-08-28 · BigCat
Antibiotics do not create resistant bacteria. The few resistant ones were already there before you dosed — you just could not tell which was which.

Selection itself is easy enough: whoever leaves more offspring passes their stuff on. But it rests on a premise that usually gets skipped — there has to be something different to pick from. If everything on the field is identical, no amount of filtering produces any change.

So what really sets how far a species can go is often not how harsh the filter is, but how many different things are on the table. Those things arrive from three sources, differing in speed by orders of magnitude — and the largest stream never passes through selection’s hands at all.

// 01

Variation comes first, and only then does filtering get a turn

Imagine copying a thick book out by hand. Over a hundred thousand words you will slip a few times. Where the slips land has nothing to do with how good the content is — you do not miscopy a passage because that passage happens to be badly written.

A cell copying that long tape is in the same position. An Icelandic study read the genomes of 78 father-mother-child trios: on average each child carried some sixty changes that neither parent had. The great majority came from the father’s side, and the count rose by roughly two for every year of his age.

The reason is concrete: eggs are essentially laid down before a woman is born, while sperm are produced continuously, and every division means recopying the whole book. More copying, more slips.

Here is the sentence that matters. Calling mutation random does not mean every position is equally likely to be miscopied — they are not. It means exactly one thing: the chance a mutation appears is unrelated to whether it would do its carrier any good. A dry year does not call forth extra drought-tolerant mutations.

That sounds like a philosophical stance; it was in fact settled by experiment. In 1943, Luria and Delbrück wanted to know: when bacteria meet a virus that kills them, are the survivors resistant because the virus provoked the change, or because they were already resistant beforehand? Both accounts predict roughly the same average number of survivors, so averages cannot separate them.

But they predict opposite things about the scatter — and scatter is countable.

If resistance appears only after dosing, each plate takes its own chances and counts should be similar; if the mutation appeared at some moment back in the tube, timing decides everything — an early one has already grown for many generations and pours out in the hundreds. What separates the two is not the average but how much the plates differ from each other.

They counted, and it was the latter. This trick of reading the scatter rather than the mean later earned a Nobel Prize in 1969, and the whole logic of antibiotic resistance rests on it today: antibiotics do not create resistant bacteria, they merely pick out the few already present and clear away all their competitors. Something starting below one in ten thousand can take over in days.

// 02

Shuffling beats printing new cards, and a hand dealt from outside beats both

Sixty changes per person sounds like a lot until you spread it over a text three billion letters long; a species cannot wait for that to accumulate. Fortunately a second route is far faster, and it produces nothing new at all — it only recombines what is already there.

When you make sperm or eggs, the set of chromosomes from your father and the set from your mother line up alongside each other, cut across, swap whole segments, then part company — in humans a few dozen swaps per round. Add that each of the 23 pairs contributes one chromosome at random, which alone gives over eight million combinations, and with the swapping layered on top, essentially every reproductive cell you make is unprecedented.

So the division of labour is clean: mutation adds new material to the pool, slowly; recombination re-deals what the pool already holds, quickly. The differences a population can offer up for filtering right now are overwhelmingly the second kind.

Recombination also does something less obvious with large consequences. In a lineage that never recombines — bacteria splitting in two, say — two good mutations landing in different individuals can never end up in the same descendant; they can only compete until one of them is gone. Switch recombination on and both can be merged. This is one mainstream answer to the question of what sex is for: it is a machine for reassembling variation that is scattered across different individuals.

The third route is fastest of all: import a whole batch of ready-made variation from another population. That is gene flow.

There is a small island in the Galápagos called Daphne Major, where the Grants watched for forty years and numbered every finch. In 1981 an immigrant male landed there — bigger than any bird on the island, singing a song nobody had heard. DNA later showed it had flown in from another island more than a hundred kilometres away, and belonged to a different species.

It paired with a local female. By the third generation, its descendants’ beaks and songs no longer matched any other bird on the island; with nobody else to pair with, they bred among themselves. Reproductive isolation that usually takes hundreds of generations was in place in three. The researchers call this line the Big Birds.

Whether that counts as a new species depends on which species definition you use — an old argument belonging to the topic on speciation. One thing is not in doubt: not one of the variants that suddenly appeared on this island arose locally.

// 03

A frequency changed does not mean somebody was picking

A severe drought around 2003 nearly wiped out the Big Birds. Two were left, a male and a female, brother and sister. When the rains returned they paired, and every Big Bird since descends from them. A lineage that had barely formed had its entire holdings compressed into whatever those two birds happened to carry — and not one of the birds the drought killed died for being insufficiently good. This is not filtering. It is sampling.

Try a more everyday picture: in a village of a few dozen households, a few surnames always vanish over a few generations — not because the people bearing them were more capable, but because of which households happened to leave descendants. Every generation is a sample drawn from the one before, and sampling carries noise of its own. This is what genetic drift means.

The backbone of the whole thing is one quantitative relation: the smaller the population, the louder the sampling noise. In a large population a variant carrying a slight advantage climbs steadily; in a small one it is often sampled away before the climb even starts. So whether selection or luck has the final word depends not on how good the variant is, but on how large the population is.

The same variant placed in two populations of different size meets completely different fates. The green line reaching the top on the left has nothing to do with being useful — it just got lucky.

And drift’s territory is startlingly large. Most mutations change nothing once made: the cell reads that tape three letters at a time with each triplet standing for one amino acid, and swapping the third letter often leaves the same amino acid — not to mention the long stretches never read out at all. Such changes have no measurable effect on whether their carrier lives or breeds, so their fate is settled almost entirely by sampling noise. Motoo Kimura pushed this to its limit in 1968: at the molecular level, the great majority of changes that end up fixed are neutral. Heresy at the time; broadly the consensus now.

Which yields the counter-intuitive corollary: seeing a variant spread through a population is not grounds for concluding it helped. Claiming selection pushed it up requires separate evidence — that it climbed faster than drift can account for, say, or that several unrelated populations each pushed the same variant up independently.

Losses from sampling are also unusually hard to repay. Cheetahs worldwide carry astonishingly little genetic difference — in the 1980s, skin grafts between unrelated cheetahs were not rejected, where any other cat would have thrown them off. Northern elephant seals were hunted down to a few dozen animals by the late nineteenth century; there are a couple of hundred thousand today, yet almost no variation shows up at the positions surveyed. The numbers came back; the variation did not.

There is a clear human example too: in an Amish community in Pennsylvania, an inherited condition involving extra fingers and short stature occurs far more often than anywhere else, and the trail leads back to one couple who arrived in 1744. This says nothing about the community being "worse"; it says how few people came in at the start. Genetic differences between human populations are in large part the residue of migration and sampling history, not a scoreboard of merit — reading them the second way is an error that was refuted over and over in the nineteenth century.

🎒 SCENES · BigCat

  • ① Engineering and systems design — an intermittent production error you cannot reproduce; two days of digging end in "probably a one-off," and it returns next month. What is stuck is not your debugging skill: the entire discussion is arguing over whether it "looks caused." Per the section on how a changed frequency does not mean somebody was picking, whether a signal can be separated at all depends on the noise floor your sample size sets, not on how big the swing looks. The changeable move: hard-code a minimum sample size into the alerting rule, below which you log but never page; then track how long it takes the same error to recur after each "one-off" verdict. Recurring faster than you predicted means it was never noise.
  • ② Writing, and this learning site itself — writing each issue’s cross-disciplinary box usually means generating and vetoing in the same breath: an idea arrives, it is not hard enough, it goes. Half an hour later the page is blank. That compresses producing and filtering into one action. The section on variation coming first before filtering gets a turn says exactly that the two are mechanically separate: error rate and filter strength are knobs you can turn independently. The changeable move: spend ten timed minutes listing candidates only, writing down even the obvious duds, with no evaluation permitted; then a separate pass that cuts on whether you can name the mechanism each one runs. Record the candidate count — below eight, the problem is on the production side.

🌀 CROSSING OVER · Connections from other fields

  • History and textual scholarship · The family tree of manuscripts: ancient texts survived by being copied out by hand, generation after generation, and a copyist’s error gets faithfully reproduced by the next one. Textual scholars build family trees of surviving manuscripts precisely from these shared errors: two copies wrong in the same place usually descend from the same ancestor. Two mechanisms from this topic are running together here — a slip has nothing to do with whether the content is right (the randomness in the section on variation coming first), while which branch survives to today often turns on which monastery escaped burning (drift). Which yields a conclusion you would not otherwise see: a variant reading being widespread is no evidence that it is closer to the original — circulation measures the survival history of copies, not the quality of the text. There is an old maxim in the discipline that says just this: manuscripts are to be weighed, not counted. Where it breaks down: a scribe understands the content, will actively "fix" passages that read wrong, and will correct against a second manuscript — which amounts to sideways flow between texts, with a definite direction, toward what reads smoothly. DNA replication has no proofreader that understands the content, so variation on the manuscript side is not purely random and the family tree is often not a clean tree at all.
  • Organisations and institutions · What hiring actually brings in: a team that takes nobody new for a few years starts producing proposals that look increasingly alike. Its members did not get duller; the set of combinable material closed. The section on shuffling beating printing gives two mechanisms: the number of combinations recombination can produce vastly exceeds the rate at which new variants appear, and in a lineage without recombination two good things sitting in two different people can never be joined. The conclusion runs against intuition: the value of a hire should be counted by how much material they bring that was not previously in the combinable pool, not by their individual ability. A senior person whose background overlaps heavily with the existing members contributes close to nothing to the combinable space, even if they are individually stronger. It also explains in passing why internal rotation is often better value than hiring — rotation is recombination, not import. Where it breaks down: in biology recombination happens automatically every generation and needs nobody’s consent, whereas every reshuffle in an organisation costs rebuilt trust and redivided authority. So the biological recombination rate is an equilibrium value tuned over the long run, while an organisation’s usually sits far below it, stuck on the fact that nobody has the authority to disturb the current division of labour — an authority problem, not a comprehension one.

Going Deeper

If most mutations are harmful, why has the mutation rate not been pushed to zero?
Pushing it down costs something: more proofreading machinery, slower copying, resources that could have gone elsewhere. Past a certain point, the gain from pushing further no longer covers the cost. There is a second limit too: a variant that lowers the mutation rate by another thousandth cannot spread at all if its benefit is smaller than the noise from drift. So the observed rate is where those two constraints leave it. Do not flip this into "organisms need variation so they keep some" — nobody is stockpiling for the future.
How do you tell whether a variant spread because it helped, or because it got lucky?
There is no clincher, but several ways to narrow it. One is speed: climbing faster than drift can explain is suspicious. Two is the neighbourhood: a variant driven up quickly by strong selection drags the whole adjacent stretch up with it, leaving a region of unusual sameness, and that sameness can be measured. Three is whether separate populations pushed the same variant up independently. The fuller reckoning belongs to the topic on adaptation and its limits.
Can gene flow wash away local adaptation that took a long time to accumulate?
Yes. Incomers bring new variation and, at the same time, combinations that do not suit the local conditions; if the incoming share is high enough, whatever was just filtered into place locally gets continuously diluted. The outcome depends on how strong the selection pressure is against how high the immigration rate is. So the same event is the fastest source of variation at one scale and the largest disturbance to local adaptation at another.
