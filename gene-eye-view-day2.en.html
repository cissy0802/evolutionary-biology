The Gene's-Eye View

26 August 2026Core LogicPhase A

The Selfish Gene may be the most badly misread title in science. It sounds like a claim about human nature; it is in fact a change of accounting units: replacing the "who" in "who does this benefit" with the information being copied rather than the body doing the copying. Changing units changes no facts, yet a whole class of ledgers that never balanced suddenly balances — and another class of phenomena, previously invisible, comes into view.

Replicator and VehicleWhat actually persists across generations

One-line intuition

A code repository versus a running process. The process holds state, can crash, and eventually exits; what gets forked elsewhere and keeps running is the stretch of code in the repo. The body is a gene's temporary runtime instance — only information is passed down.

Selective logic + mechanism

For cumulative selection to work, whatever is selected must be faithful across generations. In sexual reproduction the least faithful thing is precisely the "individual": meiosis reshuffles and recombines two genomes every generation, so your complete genome, as a combination, exists for exactly one generation and is never copied a second time. What does get copied repeatedly is a stretch of chromosome.

Williams's (1966) definition of a gene follows from this — not the transcription unit of molecular biology, but "any hereditary information for which there is a favourable or unfavourable selection bias equal to several or many times its rate of endogenous change." It is a unit whose boundaries are drawn by the recombination rate: with one to two crossovers per chromosome per human meiosis (roughly 1 cM/Mb genome-wide), a stretch of tens of kilobases can pass down nearly intact for hundreds of generations, while a whole chromosome shatters within a few.

Hull (1980) drew the distinction more cleanly still: replicator (the structure that gets copied) versus interactor (the entity that engages the environment and thereby causes differences in copying). This does not demote the body — the body is exactly the interface selection can see. It is simply not the thing that is inherited.

Across four generations recombination chops the same chromosome into ever smaller pieces — except the green stretch, which passes down whole each time. The boundary of a "gene" is drawn by the recombination rate, not by molecular biology: short enough is what makes it long-lived. (Schematic; breakpoints are arbitrary.)
Key evidence

"How long is the unit" is directly measurable: the decay of linkage disequilibrium with distance measures exactly how fast stretches get cut. The converse case is prettier — Bersaglieri et al. (2004, Am J Hum Genet) found an unusually long and unusually uniform haplotype around the lactase gene LCT in European populations, on the megabase scale. There is only one explanation: a favourable variant was driven up in frequency by strong selection faster than recombination could cut it apart. Haplotype length thereby becomes a clock for how recently selection acted.

Common misreading

"If the gene is the unit of selection, then the individual doesn't matter." This conflates two questions: what selection acts on (the interactor, usually the individual) and what accumulates across generations (the replicator, the gene). Mayr saying selection acts on individuals and Dawkins saying genes are the ultimate beneficiaries can both be true at once.

Cross-disciplinary echo

Isomorphic to "what counts as a reusable module": the boundary of a unit is defined not by the designer's intent but by how often things get copied together.

Essence: What is long-lived is neither the body nor the genome, but the stretch of information short enough to dodge the recombination scissors. Question: the units of knowledge you copy over and over — how long are they really? A sentence, or a whole methodology?

"Selfish" Is a Unit of Account, Not a Moral VerdictThe gene's-eye view as bookkeeping

One-line intuition

Replace "does this behaviour pay for this individual" with "is the total number of copies of this allele going up or down". Same ledger, different account — and a lot of behaviours that came out negative flip positive.

Selective logic + mechanism

Why bother changing units? Because on a whole class of phenomena the individual-level view computes "impossible": worker bees that never reproduce, ground squirrels giving alarm calls at the risk of being eaten. Scored as individual reproductive success, these are net losses. In copy-units the ledger balances: copies of an allele sit not only in the body carrying it but also, with probability r, in relatives. "Die to save three brothers" now comes out positive. Hamilton's (1964) rb > c is the formalisation of that conversion (the mechanism belongs to the kin selection and altruism issue).

The technical sense of "selfish" has to be nailed down: it is a statement about frequency dynamics — any allele that raises the frequency of its own copies will increase, whatever it makes its vehicle do. It carries no psychological content, and it implies nothing about how people ought to behave; the very same logic more often yields cooperation.

Does the change of units yield predictions the individual-level view cannot make? Yes — precisely where the two diverge. The mouse t haplotype is a recombination-suppressed region on chromosome 17 that heterozygous males transmit to as much as 99% of their offspring (versus the normal 50%). The mechanism is worked out in some detail: several "distorter" genes (Tcd) on t deregulate Rho/RAC signalling and impair sperm motility across the board, while only the t form of the "responder" (Tcr) rescues its own sperm (Lyon, 1984, Cell). The price is that homozygotes are sterile or die. An allele that plainly harms its vehicle, and its population, spreads anyway — the individual-level view has nothing to say about it.

Key evidence

The "green beard" was a pure thought experiment of Dawkins's: one allele producing a visible marker, the ability to recognise it, and preferential treatment of those who bear it. Keller and Ross (1998, Nature) reported a real candidate in the fire ant Solenopsis invicta: in multiple-queen colonies the egg-laying queens are all Bb heterozygotes at Gp-9, BB queens that begin to reproduce are executed by workers, and the executioners are mainly Bb workers. The later revision matters just as much: Wang et al. (2013, Nature) showed that what is at work is not a single gene but a roughly 13.8 Mb non-recombining supergene locked up by inversions (the "social chromosome"). The effect is real, but its carrier is an entire chromosomal block — which vindicates Williams in reverse: switch recombination off and the unit can grow to 13.8 Mb.

Common misreading

First, "selfish gene = human nature is selfish": from "this allele will rise in frequency" nothing follows about motives, and still less about what anyone ought to do — that is treating an is as an ought. Second, taking the view to deny that anything happens at the individual or group level; what it argues for is a choice of accounting units.

Cross-disciplinary echo

Meiosis is a fair-lottery protocol: a 50% transmission chance for every allele, and it is that fairness which keeps interests inside a genome aligned. The t haplotype is the cheating node in the protocol. Why it never took over the system has a distributed-systems flavour: cheaters that meet each other are eliminated, so the penalty scales up automatically as the frequency rises — frequency-dependent self-limitation built in, with no external enforcement.

Essence: "Selfish" describes the dynamics of copy number, not psychology and not ethics; its whole power is that it can balance ledgers the individual-level view cannot. Question: in your organisation, which local metric is playing the t haplotype — raising its own transmission rate while the whole bears the cost?

The Extended PhenotypeWhere a gene's phenotype actually ends

One-line intuition

Phenotype is not the physical notion "inside the skin" but the functional notion "as far as the gene's causal influence reaches". A library's output is not confined to its own memory space — if the caller's behaviour changes reproducibly with the library version, that too is its output.

Selective logic + mechanism

Why shouldn't the boundary be drawn at the skin? Because selection tracks exactly one thing: whether a variant in this stretch of DNA reproducibly changes the probability that it is itself copied. Whether it does so by rebuilding its own body or somebody else's makes no mathematical difference. Dawkins (1982) put it as a central theorem: an animal's behaviour tends to maximise the survival of the genes "for" that behaviour, whether or not those genes happen to be in the body performing it.

The radius of influence comes in roughly three layers: artefacts (the beaver's dam), manipulation of others (parasites rewriting host behaviour), and action at a distance (a cuckoo chick's begging call rewriting the foster parents' feeding decisions). The hardest evidence comes from manipulation. Ophiocordyceps unilateralis infects carpenter ants, drives them out of the nest, up to a particular height, and into a "death grip" on a leaf vein where they die — at a spot whose temperature and humidity happen to suit fungal fruiting and drop spores onto the trails below. The mechanical detail is more persuasive still: Fredericksen et al. (2017, PNAS) reconstructed the three-dimensional structure using serial block-face electron microscopy with deep-learning segmentation, and found fungal cells wrapping and invading muscle fibres throughout the ant's body in a connected network — while never entering the brain. Manipulation is not "seizing the central controller"; it more likely runs on secreted bioactive compounds plus direct control of muscle. The molecular intermediates are still under investigation.

The rings are a radius of causal influence, not an anatomical boundary. As long as the dashed loop closes — the effect lands on someone else, the payoff returns to your own copy number — selection can act on it, however many bodies the effect passes through.
Key evidence

The precision of the manipulation can be quantified: field surveys of death-grip positions find height, orientation and timing all strongly non-random — clustered on the undersides of leaves a few tens of centimetres above the ground, with biting concentrated around solar noon (Andersen et al., 2009; Hughes et al., 2011). Matching the transmission requirements that precisely is exactly what distinguishes "adaptive manipulation" from "side effect of being ill".

The counterexample has to be given too. Toxoplasma gondii abolishing rodents' aversion to cat odour was the textbook case of "fatal attraction" (Berdoy et al., 2000). But Boillat et al. (2020, Cell Reports) found that infected mice show a general drop in anxiety — aversion to fox and guinea-pig odours weakens as well — which looks more like a by-product of neuroinflammation. The framework is solid; whether a given case is manipulation or pathology has to be judged one case at a time.

Common misreading

Treating it as a mystical claim about "remote control". It introduces no new causal force; it only redraws the statistical unit — if the association "genotype → another organism's behaviour" is stable and heritable, selection can act on it. The other misreading is to score every parasite-induced behavioural change as adaptive manipulation: being ill changes behaviour all by itself.

Cross-disciplinary echo

The same point as "the interface boundary is not the causal boundary" in engineering: a module's real output is whatever observables it changes, not what lives in its address space. The counterpart inside biology is niche construction — organisms remodel their environment, and the remodelled environment becomes the selective pressure their descendants face.

Essence: A phenotype ends where the causal influence ends, not where the body carrying the gene ends. Question: for the thing you build, does the most important output happen inside your "boundary" or outside it?

Levels of Selection: The Decomposition Is Not UniquePrice's equation and what the argument is really about

One-line intuition

The same total can be broken out into different accounts. Price's equation is that decomposition operator: it splits the total change in a population trait exactly into a "between-group" and a "within-group" term — change the split and the total is unchanged to the last decimal.

Selective logic + mechanism

Price's (1970) equation reads w̄·Δz̄ = Cov(w, z) + E(w·Δz): the first term is selection (the covariance of the trait with fitness), the second is transmission. Its power lies in being recursively expandable: nest individuals inside groups and the selection term splits into "between-group covariance + average within-group covariance" — which is precisely the formalisation of multilevel selection. From this follows a conclusion both camps routinely abuse: inclusive fitness and correctly specified multilevel selection are two ways of writing the same decomposition (Hamilton did the conversion himself in 1975; Okasha systematised it in 2006). So "which level is the real one" is a defective question — equivalent notations cannot disagree about facts.

What genuine disagreement remains? Empirical and modelling-strategy questions: how large between-group variance actually is in a given system (measurable), and which style of bookkeeping is less error-prone. In 2010 Nowak, Tarnita and Wilson argued in Nature that inclusive fitness is only a limited-scope approximation; the following year 137 authors published a joint rebuttal in the same journal (Abbot et al., 2011). It has not converged.

A distinction worth holding onto: modern multilevel selection is not naive group selection. "For the good of the species" is wrong not because group-level selection is mathematically impossible, but because it requires between-group variance to exceed within-group variance and group turnover to outrun the spread of free-riders inside groups — conditions rarely satisfied together (Williams, 1966).

Key evidence

Group-level selection does work when the conditions hold: Wade (1977) ran artificial group-selection experiments on flour beetles (Tribolium), selecting on whole-group adult counts, and got a clear response — but the conditions assembled in the lab (small groups, frequent re-founding, minimal migration) rarely co-occur in the wild. The argument thereby shifts from "is it possible" to "how common is it".

On the other side, the gene's-eye view has made quantitative predictions that were then tested: Trivers and Hare (1976, Science) derived from the asymmetry between a worker's relatedness to sisters versus brothers that colonies with a singly mated queen should bias investment in brood towards females, close to 3:1, and saw the skew in multi-species data. Larger later samples show the actual ratio varying systematically with queen mating frequency: the direction matches theory, but 3:1 holds only under specific conditions.

Common misreading

"The gene's-eye view won and group selection was refuted." What was refuted is a particular version of naive group selection — traits evolving for the good of the group with no variance condition required. The reverse misreading is just as common: "if the maths is equivalent, write it however you like." Equivalent is not equally usable; a bad decomposition inverts the causal story — reading a group's decline under spreading free-riders, say, as the group "sacrificing itself".

Cross-disciplinary echo

Isomorphic to credit assignment in reinforcement learning: a trajectory's total return can be split by timestep or by sub-policy, with the total identical and learning efficiency wildly different — non-uniqueness of the decomposition is a mathematical fact, choosing one is an engineering judgement. Much of the levels-of-selection argument is a judgement dispute mistaken for a factual one.

Essence: Asking which level selection acts on has an answer only relative to the decomposition you chose; what can be settled empirically is which level's variance is driving the change. Question: when you attribute a failure, is your breakdown the only possible one — or just the one you know best?

Going Deeper

If the boundary of a "gene" is drawn by recombination, what is left of the gene's-eye view where there is no recombination?
Asexual organisms, mitochondrial genomes, the Y chromosome, supergenes locked by inversions — all places where recombination is switched off. The whole block becomes a single replicator and the gene's-eye and individual-level views collapse into the same thing, which shows that the view's explanatory power comes from recombination rather than from the molecular sense of the word "gene". The price shows up too: a non-recombining block cannot separate harmful from beneficial mutations and accumulates them together (Muller's ratchet), of which the degeneration of the Y chromosome is the long-run result.
Where does the extended phenotype stop? Do artefacts and culture count?
Two criteria, both required: a stable association between the effect and heritable variation, and a payoff that returns to the copy number of that variation. A beaver dam qualifies — dam structure varies with genetic variation and the return lands on the builder's reproduction. Human cities do not: the variation travels culturally rather than genetically, so you need the models of cultural evolution instead (the gene-culture coevolution issue works those through). The controversy over memes starts exactly here: borrowing the form does not inherit the mathematical consequences.
The gene's-eye view predicts things the individual-level view cannot. Does that make it "more true"?
It makes it more useful in those places, not truer everywhere. The divergence points — meiotic drive, genomic imprinting, green beards — are its home ground. There are places where it runs the other way: phenotypic plasticity, the integrated regulation of development, where phrasing things in terms of "copy number of an allele" gets extremely awkward. Treat the view as a tool rather than an ontology and there is no need to take sides.
